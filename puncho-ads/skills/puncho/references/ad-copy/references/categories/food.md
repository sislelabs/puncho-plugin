# Food, drink and everyday products

Read this for any brand that sells something people eat, drink or use daily. It
replaces the young-audience defaults in the main skill where they disagree.

## Appetite first, virtue second

People buy food because they want to eat it. Health, provenance and ethics
make the choice feel right, but they seldom create the wanting. In a feed, the
hook's job is to make someone hungry or to remind them of a moment. The virtue
goes in the primary text, where it closes the sale.

What to push against, in rough order of power for this category:

1. **A moment the reader already has.** A time of day, an occasion, a place in
   the house where the product actually gets eaten, named exactly enough that
   the reader recognises their own.
2. **A sensory gap.** The cracker that goes soft, the spread that tastes of
   sugar, the drink that tastes of nothing. Name what the reader can taste or
   hear.
3. **A habit, gently mocked.** Something buyers of this product really do and
   half-admit. The reader laughs because they do it too. They are in on the
   joke, never the butt of it.
4. **A category default, beaten visibly.** Real butter where the category uses
   vegetable fat, shown or named, never with a competitor's name.
5. **Provenance.** Real, and strong as proof. Weak as a hook for a stranger,
   because a stranger does not yet care where it comes from.

## Make it concrete

Sensory words carry food copy, but only specific ones. "Delicious" and
"quality" draw nothing. A sound, a texture, a temperature, a crumb: things a
reader can picture. A food hook with no
noun you could photograph fails "can you see it?".

## Formats that work in this category

- **Close-up texture.** The snap, the pour, the spread. Here the picture is the
  hook, and the words only say when this happens.
- **Evidence of eating.** What is left after the product is gone. It proves the
  product is good without saying so.
- **Confession.** The brand admits the human thing. Self-mockery is what makes an
  honest brand sound honest: Oatly built a category on it.
- **Challenge.** The reader does something right now, in their own kitchen, and
  the comparison happens there rather than in the ad.
- **Split the room.** A question people take sides on. Proven in organic.
  In paid it needs the product in the picture, or it is a poll with no seller.
- **Occasion.** Tied to a season, a holiday, a routine: Easter, the first
  picnic, the school run.

The product is always the brand's real photograph (`PRODUCT_SHOT`), never drawn.
What can be drawn is everything around it: the dish it makes, the table, the
hands, the light. Generated parts around the real cut-out pack (the dish,
the surface, a hand reaching in) are the default picture for a food ad: write
that into the visual idea. The picture carries the appetite, so the hook only
has to name the moment.

## The crowded claims

In most food markets the loudest ads all list what the product leaves out: "100%
natural", "no sugar", "no preservatives", "bio". Record the local list here
when research finds it, with the date. A core hook avoids it by default; the
human can overrule that, and an experiment may test it on purpose.

- **Bulgaria, 2026-09 (research for an organic food brand):** "100% натурално /
  100% български", "Без захар. Без консерванти.", "Истински … без компромиси",
  БИО as a badge. Nobody was funny, nobody named a person, nobody talked about
  eating.

## Claim rules (EU)

These decide what a food hook may say at all. Raise doubts with the client
rather than softening a line until it says nothing.

- **Health claims** (Reg. 1924/2006): "healthy", "good for you", "boosts
  energy" or "for immunity" need an authorised claim behind them. Leave them
  out. Say what is in the product and let the reader conclude.
- **Nutrition claims** ("no added sugar", "high in protein") have legal
  thresholds. Use one only if the client confirms the product meets it.
  Naming a nutrient as a benefit is one too: "all nine essential amino
  acids", "rich in vitamins", "a source of fibre". A number from the label
  ("20 g protein a serving") is not: prefer it. Lines with these words are
  flagged for whoever approves.
- **Don't advertise what every product has** (Reg. 1169/2011, Art. 7): "no
  preservatives" on a product that never needs them is misleading.
- **"Bio" / organic** (Reg. 2018/848) only for certified products, and only as
  certified.
- **Provenance** ("from Bulgarian hazelnuts", "our own farms") must be true for
  every ingredient the line covers.

## Worked example: an organic food brand's first drafts

The first drafts, and the test each one failed. The fixes are named as moves on
purpose: write the line for the product in front of you.

| Draft | Failed | The move instead |
|---|---|---|
| „Соленките, в които маслото е масло." | could only we sign it? (the purity claim everyone makes); symmetry | challenge: the reader checks the category default themselves |
| „Лешници от България. И толкова." | crowded claim; two-beat | confession: a habit buyers of a spread share |
| „Лъжицата е за тахана. Филийката е по избор." | symmetry; a translated meme | the same idea, said plainly |
| „Колко топчета има в пакета? Още никой не е стигнал до края." | could only we sign it? (every snack is moreish) | a named moment: who finishes the pack, and when |
| „Може ли течният шоколад да е полезен?" | a health claim, as a question; the ad never showed the product | say what is in the jar; show the jar |
