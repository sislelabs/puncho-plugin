# The format library

Shapes taken from references that already worked, with what each one costs to
pull off. Shape transfers across categories; words do not. A beauty listicle and
a hackathon listicle work for the same reason — neither looks like an ad.

Keep this file honest: add a shape when a reference on the board demonstrates it,
and record where it came from.

---

## Screenshot-native

*Seen in: the "5 secret setting spray hacks" reference (beauty).*

A numbered list typed in a phone notes app. Emoji per line. One item highlighted
as if by the author. The creator's face creeping into a corner, uncropped.

**Why it works:** it does not look like an ad, so it is read before it is
classified as one. The scroll stops because it looks like something a friend
screenshotted.

**Requires:** list-shaped content — five of something, genuinely. The moment it
is three padded items it reads as an ad imitating a screenshot, which is worse
than an honest ad.

**Fails when:** the brand's logo appears, the typography is too good, or the list
is obviously marketing copy in a notes-app costume.

## POV / named feeling

*Seen in: the Uber Eats reference.*

"POV:" plus a feeling the viewer has had this week, over real footage. The
product arrives as the answer and is never the subject.

**Why it works:** it names a private feeling precisely enough that being seen is
the reward. Recognition, not persuasion.

**Requires:** a feeling specific enough to sting. "When you're tired" is nothing.
"a hell of a week and it's only Tuesday" is dated, exact and true.

**Fails when:** the feeling is generic, or the product shows up too early.

## Struck-through past

*Seen in: the Lovable reference.*

The old cost, crossed out. The new reality beside it, large. Often over real
product UI with the cursor left visible.

**Why it works:** the strikethrough does the arguing before a word is read, and
the visible cursor says "in use" rather than "for sale".

**Requires:** a real before — a price, a duration, a requirement that genuinely
used to apply. Inventing one is a fabricated claim, not a creative device.

**Fails when:** the "before" is a straw man the audience never believed.

## You versus them

*Seen in: the Wise reference.*

Two columns. The status quo and the alternative, with a visible loser — a red ✗
against a green ✓.

**Why it works:** the layout scores the argument before the copy is read.

**Requires:** a difference that survives scrutiny, and an antagonist you are
willing to name. Also the nerve to show a competitor losing.

**Fails when:** the comparison is unfair in a way the audience can spot, which
transfers their scepticism onto everything else you claim.

## Unscripted reaction

*Seen in: the street vox-pop reference ("You can taste the popcorn").*

A real stranger's first reaction, caught handheld, subtitle burned in.

**Why it works:** obviously unscripted. The quote is the whole ad, and its
imperfection is the proof.

**Requires:** actual footage of an actual person. This shape cannot be
synthesised — a generated "candid reaction" is a lie the audience reads
instantly. For anything people attend or taste in person, it is also the
easiest shape to shoot for real.

---

## Peer reframe

*Seen in: one project's own organic (an event for teenagers) — famous founders
under a line saying they were once just kids with ideas.*

Famous people, cut out hard, recast as peers of the audience.

**Why it works:** closes the distance between the reader and a status they want.
It flatters without praising, which is the only flattery that lands on teenagers.

**Requires:** faces the audience actually recognises, and a line that does the
reframing rather than just captioning the picture.

**Note:** real people's likenesses in paid ads carry a risk that organic does
not. Worth checking before spending behind it.
